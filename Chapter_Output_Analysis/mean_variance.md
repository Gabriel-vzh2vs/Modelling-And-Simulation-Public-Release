## A more general setting for systems and output analysis

Here we consider the case where we have a system $S$, a model $M$, and
simulation model $\mathcal{M}$. We have $m$ measures of interest that
we denote by $X_k$ for $1\le k \le m$. Each run of the simulation
model $\mathcal{M}$ outputs a value for each of the $X_k$'s.

In general, $S$ will have stochastic elements which we capture through
random variables in $M$, each with their specific distributions,
perhaps derived as in {ref}`chap:distribution_modeling`. This means
that the measures $X_k$ themselves are generally random variables. If
we use different random seeds to initialize our simulation instances,
we would normally expect that the outputs we generate will help us estimate the
joint distribution of the $X_k$'s as well as related random variables.

```{note} __Perspective:__
You may consider the model itself as some sort of
complex random variable defined over a probability space (likely
a complicated one). In this setting, running simulations amounts to sampling
this random variable. As we will see in the section
{ref}`chap:random_number_generation`, a good understanding of random
number generation is important to ensure that sampling is done rigorously.
An advanced simulation scientist may also care about sampling _efficiently_, perhaps
using _variance reduction techniques_ which we cover in its own chapter.
```

In almost all cases, we will run our simulation $\mathcal{M}$ many
times. Each run generates an output for each of the measures we are
watching, and here we introduce notation for tracking this. First we write

$$
X_k = (X_{k,1}, X_{k,2}, \ldots, X_{k,m}) \;.
$$

for the vector of outputs generated by the $k$th simulation. Thus,
$X_{3,2}$ denotes the 2nd measure of the 3rd simulation. If you do not
want to confuse $X_k$ (the vector) and the $k$th measures, you may
want to write $\bm{X}_k$ for the vector. This is generally clear from
the context, so we will only do this when there is ambiguity. Note
again that the $X_{k,i}$'s generally are random variables in this
book.

The values (or outcomes) we obtain for the $k$th simulation (or
experiment) are denoted

$$
x_k = (x_{k,1}, x_{k,2}, \ldots, x_{k,m}) \;.
$$

The convention of using uppercase letters for random variables and
matching lowercase letters for their realizations is standard
practice. We note that throughout this book, we will assume that the
random variables are real-valued (yes, there are other possibilities).

With this we can now summarize the outcomes of $n$ simulations, each
with $m$ measures, as a matrix:

\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:outcome_matrix}
\begin{matrix}
  \text{Measure:}\quad & 1 & 2 & \ldots & m \\
  \text{Run 1:}\quad & x_{1,1} & x_{1_2} & \ldots & x_{1,m}  \\
  \text{Run 2:}\quad & x_{2,1} & x_{2_2} & \ldots & x_{2,m}  \\
    & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
  \text{Run $n$:}\quad & x_{n,1} & x_{n_2} & \ldots & x_{n,m}  \\

\end{matrix}
\end{equation}


__Question:__ What if the measure $X_{k,i}$ is a time series and not
just a single number? An example of this is where $X_{k,i}$ is the
list of arrival times of each customer at the Ivy Post Office as
simulated in run $k$. That would add one more dimension to the matrix
in {ref}`eq:outcome_matrix`. We will address this later, bringing in
the context of stochastic process.


## Estimating Means ##

We consider the base case where we have a single performance measure
captured as a random variable $X$ in our model $M$.
We have run
the simulation model $N$ times, collected the output, and constructed a
sample

$$
 Z = \{x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_N\} \;.
$$

We would now like to estimate $\mu = \mathbb{E}[X]$. The obvious
candidate for $\mu$ is the average of $Z$. This is generally works
out, a consequence of the:

```{prf:theorem} The Strong Law of Large Numbers (SLLN)
For any sequence $X_1$, $X_2$, $X_3$, $\ldots$ of independent, identically
distributed random variables with finite expectation $\mu$, we have with probability 1
\begin{equation*}
\lim_{n\to \infty} \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i = E(X)\;.
\end{equation*}
Stated differently:
\begin{equation*}
\Pr\Bigl(\lim_{n\to \infty} \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i = E(X)\Bigr) = 1\;
\end{equation*}
```

```{exercise}
You have a strange 23-sided die with sides labeled 1 through
23. You want to estimate the odds of rolling a 17. You let $X_k$
denote the random variable capturing the outcome of experiment/roll
$k$. Are the conditions in the SLLN met?
```

```{exercise}
Kroger is your client. They report too long lines at
register~11. You, the consultant, decide to start by estimating
average queue length at register 11. For this, you let $X_k$ for $k=0$
through $k=15$ denote the queue length at hour $8 + k$ using military
time.
\begin{equation*}
  \text{To SLLN or not to SLLN?}
\end{equation*}
```


How does the SLLN apply to simulations? Let us continue the case with
a single measure, and let us simply write $X_k$ rather than $X_{k,1}$
for this random variable capturing the $k$th simulation. The
realization (or output value) for the $k$th simulation is thus
$x_k$. The SLLN allows us to use

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mean}
 \bar{X}(N) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N X_i
\end{equation}

for estimating the mean $\mu = \mathbb{E}[X]$. But we have to make
sure that the $X_k$'s are independent.

## The random variable $\bar{X}(n)$

We note that $\bar{X}(n)$ is a random variable. Why? It is a sum of
random variables. As such, and with the assumptions on $\mu(X)$ and
the variance $\sigma^2(X)$ being finite, $\bar{X}(n)$ has expectation
and variance. We want to know what these are.

A quick calculation using linearity of expectation shows that
\begin{equation*}
\mathbb{E}[\bar{X}(n)]
= \mathbb{E}[\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n X_i]
= \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{E}[X_i]
= \frac{1}{n} n \, \mu = \mu\;.
\end{equation*}

Since the expectation of the estimator equals $\mu(X)$ we call
$\bar{X}$ __unbiased__ . The variance of $\bar{X}(n)$ is can be
determined in a similar way:

\begin{equation*}
\mathbb{V}[\bar{X}(n)]
= \mathbb{V}[\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n X_i]
= \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{V}[X_i]
= \frac{1}{n^2} n\, \sigma^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}
\end{equation*}

The second step uses independence as well as the fact that
$\mathbb{V}[aX] = a^2 \mathbb{V}[X]$ for a constant $a$. Note the
following: the variance involves $\sigma^2(X)$ which we do not
have. In general, we will need to estimate this quantity.


__Estimating $\sigma^2(X)$__ The __sample variance__ of $X$  is

\begin{equation}
 S^2(n) = \frac{1}{n-1} \sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X}(n))^2 \;,
\end{equation}
which is also a random variable. It is an unbiased estimator since
$\mathbb{E}[S^2(n)] = \sigma^2$. (Exercise: show this)
Combining with the above we obtain the estimator for the variance of $\bar{X}(n)$:

\begin{equation*}
\widehat{\mathbb{V}}[\bar{X}(n)]
= \frac{S^2(n)}{n}
= \frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X}(n))^2
\end{equation*}

For example, if $n$ is large and one naively [sic] adds up the
terms in $S^2(n)$ one may get to the point where the contribution of
the next term in the sum is neglible compared to the running
sum. Underflow occurs. If you keep adding you also risk overflow. What
are better ways? Here are some comments and thoughts.

- If you write your own production code, you should likely avoid the
for-loop approach, see for example the [Kahan
algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kahan_summation_algorithm)
and note the remark on compiler optimization.

- If you use libraries (e.g., Python) you may want to check the
documentation carefully. It may also be instructing to check the
actual implementation, should you have access to this.

- Sometimes you may have ot peform an online computation where data comes from a
stream and you do not have access to all the data at once.


When is this a problem when working in Excel? Note that this is not
specific to Excel, but something you may have to think about for any
tool or software package.

- The "big data" problem: You may run out of memory trying to load or

  numerical shortcomings.  Do we still need to be careful?

\begin{frame}
\frametitle{\S 4.5 Confidence intervals and hypothesis testing for the
  mean}

\hmbullet We have seen how to estimate the mean using $\bar{X}$. The
approach is anchored in the strong law of large numbers.

\hmbullet In addition to $\mu$, we also need to provide a guarantee
that our estimate is good, or some measure that allows us to quantify its quality.

\hmbullet The standard approach is to construct \textbf{confidence
  intervals} for the mean: determine real values $a$ and $b$ such that
we can state $a\le \mu \le b$ with a prescribed probability.

\hmbullet The approach is based on the:

\textbf{Central Limit Theorem (CLT):}
Let $X_1$, $X_2$, $X_3$, $\ldots$ be a sequence of IID random
variables with finite mean $\mu$ and finite standard deviation
$\sigma^2 > 0$, and let $Z_n$ be the random variable
\begin{equation*}
  Z_n = (\bar{X} - \mu)/\sqrt{\sigma^2/n} \;.
\end{equation*}
Then
\begin{equation*}
\Pr( Z_n \le z ) = F_{n,Z}(z)
\longrightarrow
\Phi(z) \quad \text{as $n\to \infty$}\;.
\end{equation*}
Here $\Phi$ is the distribution function of the standard normal
distribution ($\mu = 0$, $\sigma^2=1$).
\end{frame}

%% ----------------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{frame}
\frametitle{Confidence intervals for $\mu$ - Round 1}

\hmbullet Before we can proceed we need to address the fact that $Z_n$
contains $\sigma^2$, and that \textbf{we do not know $\sigma^2$}.

\hmbullet We use the estimator $S^2(n)$ and introduce the new random
variable
\begin{equation*}
t_n = (\bar{X}(n) - \mu)/\sqrt{S^2(n)/n}
\end{equation*}
which, by the CLT, has, approximately, the standard normal
distribution when $n$ is ``sufficiently large''. (Yes, what is
sufficiently large?  Stay tuned.)

\hmbullet Armed with this, we can now state that, for sufficiently
large $n$, we \textbf{approximately} have:
\begin{align*}
 \Pr\Bigl(
 & -z_{1-\alpha/2} \le (\bar{X}(n) - \mu)/\sqrt{S^2(n)/n} \le z_{1-\alpha/2}
  \Bigr)
  \\
&=
\Pr\left(
\bar{X}(n) -z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{S^2(n)/n}
\le - \mu
\le \bar{X}(n) + z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{S^2(n)/n}
\right) \\
&\approx 1 - \alpha
\end{align*}

\hmbullet $\alpha$ is often called the \textbf{significance level})

\hmbullet The quantity $z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{S^2(n)/n}$ is called the
\textbf{half length} of the confidence interval.
\end{frame}

%% ----------------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{frame}
\frametitle{Confidence intervals}

\hmbullet The confidence interval is:
\begin{equation}
[
  \bar{X}(n) - z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{S^2(n)/n},
  \bar{X}(n) + z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{S^2(n)/n}
]
\end{equation}

\hmbullet How does one interpret confidence intervals?

\hmbullet For fixed $n$ and $\alpha$: if one constructs a large number
of independent $100(1-\alpha)$ confidence intervals, the fraction of
these intervals that contain (or cover) $\mu$ should be
$(1-\alpha)$. This fraction (or proportion) is called the
\textbf{coverage} for the confidence interval.

\hmbullet In other words: in your output analysis, when you have your
confidence interval, you are providing \textbf{a probabilistic
  guarantee}.

\hmbullet You may want to look at Law Example 4.26.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
\frametitle{Confidence intervals for $\mu$ - Round 2}

\hmbullet Knowing when ``$n$ is sufficiently large'' presents an issue.

\hmbullet The more \textbf{skewed} the underlying distribution of the
$X_i$'s, the more of an issue it becomes, and the larger $n$ will
generally have to be.

\hmbullet \textbf{Step 1:}
\begin{itemize}
\item He considers the case where the $X_i$'s are, in fact, normally
  distributed.
\item In this case,
  \begin{equation*}
   t_n = (\bar{X}(n) - \mu)/\sqrt{S^2(n)/n}>
  \end{equation*}
  has a Student's $t$-distribution with $n-1$ degrees of freedom for
  which one has the exact confidence interval:
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:student_t_interval}
\bar{X}(n) \pm t_{n-1,1-\alpha/2} \sqrt{S^2(n)/n}
  \end{equation}
\item This interval is \emph{wider} (or somewhat more conservative)
  than the one constructed earlier. (For large $n$, e.g., $n\ge 100$, the difference is small)
\item Law's recommendation is to use the confidence interval~\eqref{eq:student_t_interval}.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}
\frametitle{Confidence intervals for $\mu$ - The finale!}

\hmbullet The Willink confidence interval (p. 237) is:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:willink_interval}
[
  \bar{X}(n) - G(t_{n-1,1-\alpha/2})\sqrt{S^2(n)/n},
  \bar{X}(n) + G(t_{n-1,1-\alpha/2})\sqrt{S^2(n)/n}
]
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align*}
G(r) &=
  (1+6a(r-a)^{1/3} - 1)/(2a)\\
a &=
  \frac{\hat{\mu}_3}{6\sqrt{n}(S^2(n))^{3/2}}\;,\text{ and}\\
\hat{\mu}_3 &=
  \frac{n}{(n-1)(n-1)} \sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X}(n))^3 \;.
\end{align*}

\hmbullet General philosophy regarding results like~\eqref{eq:willink_interval}.

\begin{frame}
\frametitle{Hypothesis testing regarding $\mu$}

\hmbullet Setting:
\begin{itemize}
\item We assume the $X_i$'s are approximately normally distributed, and
\item have $\mu_0$ as a fixed hypothesized value for $\mu$, and
\item we want to test the null hypothesis $H_0\colon \mu = \mu_0$
  against the alternative hypothesis $H_1 \colon \mu \ne \mu_0$.
\item Under the assumption that $H_0$ is true, the test statistic
  \begin{equation*}
   t_n = (\bar{X}(n) - \mu_0)/\sqrt{S^2(n)/n}
  \end{equation*}
  has a Student's $t$-distribution with $n-1$ degrees of freedom.
\item The two-tailed hypothesis test for $H_0$ is to reject $H_0$ if
  $|t_n| > t_{n-1,1-\alpha_2}$ and fail to reject $H_0$ otherwise.
\end{itemize}
You will recognize this from Statistics. Please review as
necessary. In 3062, we will focus on confidence intervals.
